1. The stack: one pixel's journey

Nothing about game art scale makes sense as a single number. A sprite passes through five distinct coordinate systems between the file on disk and your perception of it, and each hop multiplies or divides. The whole craft of "getting zoom right" is making those multiplications land on numbers the eye likes.

The five stages, with our real numbers

Stage 1 -- source art. The house standard (set by our PixelLab generation convention: create_character, size 64, "low top-down") emits 68x68 px PNGs, of which the opaque subject -- the actual cat or person, not the transparent padding -- is roughly 34-50 px tall. Floor tilesets arrive as 128x128 atlases of 32 px tiles. One orphan exception: the current office worker (artloop_char) is 180x180 with a ~140 px subject, matching nothing else in the library. That mismatch caused this morning's giant-person bug, and section 3 explains the cleanup plan.

Stage 2 -- game scale constants. Code multiplies source pixels onto the game's canvas. Floor tiles: 32 px source x FLOOR_TILE_SCALE = 2 = 64 px on the canvas. That "64 px tile" is the single most important number in the whole system -- section 4 builds everything from it. Characters get SPRITE_SCALE, cats get their own factor. (That these are scattered constants rather than one system is a known limitation; a Camera2D would make this stage a single tunable. See section 8.)

Stage 3 -- the virtual viewport. The game declares a 1920x1080 design canvas (project.godot: stretch mode canvas_items, aspect expand). Everything in stage 2 is positioned in these "virtual pixels". This canvas is what screenshots capture and what all our numbers so far live in.

Stage 4 -- the monitor. The viewport gets stretched to the player's actual screen. On a 1080p monitor the mapping is 1:1 -- one virtual pixel is one physical pixel. On a 1440p monitor everything is scaled x1.333 uniformly. Physical pixel size depends on the panel: a 24-inch 1080p panel has pixels ~0.277 mm wide; a 27-inch 1440p panel ~0.233 mm. (Worked in section 2.)

Stage 5 -- the eye. What the eye cares about is not millimetres but visual angle: how much of your field of view the thing occupies, measured in degrees and arcminutes (1 degree = 60 arcminutes). A 20/20 eye resolves detail down to about 1 arcminute -- that is the floor under every readability decision we make.

One number, all the way down

Follow a 2-tile-tall character (our post-fix target) through every stage, assuming a 24-inch 1080p monitor at 60 cm viewing distance:

source subject ~68 px canvas, subject ~64 px tall (regenerated at house standard) game scale 64 x 2.0 = 128 canvas px (= exactly 2 floor tiles) viewport 128 px of 1080 = 11.9% of screen height monitor 128 x 0.277 mm = ~35 mm tall on the glass eye 35.4 / 600 rad = ~3.4 degrees of visual angle per source pixel 2 physical px = ~0.55 mm = ~3.2 arcminutes -- comfortably readable

Every design question in this document is some version of: pick the stage, do this arithmetic, check the bottom line against what eyes can actually see.

2. The eye math

Two facts about hardware, one about biology, and everything else follows.

Fact 1: physical pixel size

A 16:9 monitor's width is its diagonal x 16/√(16²+9²) = diagonal x 0.8716. Divide by horizontal resolution:

24" 1080p: 609.6 mm x 0.8716 = 531 mm wide / 1920 px = 0.277 mm per px 27" 1440p: 685.8 mm x 0.8716 = 598 mm wide / 2560 px = 0.233 mm per px

Fact 2: visual angle at desk distance

At a typical desktop viewing distance of ~60 cm (approximate -- people sit anywhere from 50 to 75 cm), one physical pixel subtends:

angle = pixel_size / distance (radians, small-angle) 24" 1080p: 0.277 / 600 = 4.6e-4 rad = ~1.6 arcminutes 27" 1440p: 0.233 / 600 = 3.9e-4 rad = ~1.3 arcminutes

Fact 3: the eye's resolution

Normal (20/20) acuity resolves about 1 arcminute of detail. So on common desktop setups, a single physical pixel sits just above the threshold of perception -- visible as a dot, but carrying almost no shape information. For a feature to read as a shape (an eye, a coffee mug handle, a cat's ear) it needs to span roughly 2-3 physical pixels minimum.

The craft consequences.
  • 1 source pixel at x2 = ~3 arcminutes. This is why pixel art shown at 2x feels crisp rather than either mushy or strained: each art pixel is a fat, clearly-visible chunk, ~3x the eye's threshold.
  • A character eye drawn as 1 source pixel (2 physical px at our scale) is at the edge of reading as a feature. Expressions are impossible at in-game scale; identity must come from silhouette, palette, and animation. Faces are the hero library's job (the SEED_ART_DIRECTION "fidelity split": hero-hardcore for close-ups, in-game-cozy for the floor).
  • Below ~48 screen px of character height, silhouette carries identity (craft rule of thumb, not a law). Our 128 px characters are safely above this; our old ~15 px cat was far below it -- it read as a smudge.

3. Integer scaling, and why 0.91x is a compromise

Pixel art has a property photographs don't: every pixel is a deliberate decision, and the grid itself is part of the aesthetic. That makes the scale factor between source and screen matter enormously.

Why whole numbers are magic

Nearest-neighbour scaling (the only correct filter for pixel art -- anything smoothing turns crisp art to blur) works by mapping each screen pixel back to whichever source pixel it lands on. At 2x, every source pixel becomes an exact 2x2 block: the art is simply bigger, perfectly faithful. At a non-integer factor like 1.5x, the mapping cannot be even: some source pixels become 2 screen px wide and their neighbours only 1. Straight lines get lumpy. Worse, the lumps move: as a sprite walks across the screen, which pixels get doubled depends on where the sprite sits on the grid, so edges shimmer and "swim" frame to frame. The eye is exquisitely sensitive to that kind of crawling motion.

Downscaling is worse: rows simply vanish

Scaling down past 1x doesn't squeeze pixels, it deletes them. The interim fix for the giant office worker runs the 180 px orphan art at ~0.91x: 180 rows of source have to land on ~164 rows of screen, so roughly 1 row in every 11 is silently dropped. Which rows drop depends on grid position -- so a walking character's outline boils slightly, and hand-placed single-pixel details (a tie clip, an eye highlight) can blink out of existence entirely.

Honest status of today's fix. Setting SPRITE_SCALE to ~0.91 on the 180 px art chooses correct world size (a 2-tile person) at the cost of pixel integrity (dropped rows, mild shimmer). On a small inset office view this is an acceptable interim. The clean end-state is already agreed: regenerate the office worker at the 68 px house standard and display at exactly 2x (= 136 px, ~2.1 tiles), same as the cats, the cast characters, and the tiles. One art family, one integer factor, no orphans.

The stretch layer on top

There is one more scaling stage you don't control: the player's monitor. Our 1920x1080 viewport maps 1:1 on a 1080p panel, but on 1440p the OS/engine stretches the whole frame x1.333 -- a non-integer factor again. Why is this tolerable when per-sprite 1.5x isn't? Because it is global and uniform: everything on screen shares the same distortion, nothing shimmers relative to anything else, and the sprite never moves across a fixed sampling grid -- the grid moves with the frame. It softens the image slightly; it doesn't make it crawl. Games that care to the last percent offer integer-scaling display modes with black borders; ours is a UI-heavy strategy game and the canvas_items stretch is the right default. Know the layer exists, then stop worrying about it.

4. World proportions: the tile is the unit

Stop thinking in pixels for a moment. Inside the game world the natural unit is the floor tile (64 canvas px). Every object's size should be a deliberate number of tiles, because the eye judges scale relationally -- a person is "the right size" only relative to the door, the desk, the cat.

No player consciously computes "6.7 cooler-heights"; they just feel doll-house -- giants in a miniature room. The ensemble ratio is what reads, which is also why fixing only the human without re-proportioning props would still feel wrong: scale is a property of the scene, not of any sprite.

Where the numbers live going forward. The prop manifest (in flight) records each prop's height_tiles and footprint_tiles, replacing the old force-everything-to-46px rule. Character and cat scale reduce to one shared "pixels per tile" logic. When your eye disagrees with a number in the compare sandbox, change the manifest value -- not a scattered constant.

A tension to keep one eye on: pixel density

If cats (68 px source, ~34 px subject) display near 1x while humans (68 px source, ~64 px subject) display at 2x, then a cat's art-pixels are physically half the size of a human's art-pixels on screen. Mixed pixel grain is a classic pixel-art smell -- it reads as "assets from two different games". Options, in rising effort: accept it (small objects get away with finer grain more than large ones do); generate small creatures on proportionally smaller canvases so everything displays at the same 2x; or treat 1x-vs-2x as a deliberate two-density system (background grain vs foreground grain). This is exactly the "pixel-density review" flagged in the pre-WS-3 art ramp -- decide it once, write it in the art direction doc, and the generation pipeline enforces it thereafter.

5. Perspective: what "low top-down" buys and costs

Our art is generated in PixelLab's "low top-down" view -- the classic 3/4 perspective of 2D RPGs and sims. It is worth understanding what that actually encodes, because it is baked into every sprite pixel and is therefore the single most expensive thing to ever change.

What it buys. Floors become visible area (you can see where things are -- essential for an office sim), while characters and props still show their recognisable front faces. Verticals are compressed -- a 1.75 m person does not get a full 1.75 m worth of pixels, because part of their screen height budget is "spent" on the viewing angle -- and that compression is why a 2-tile person over a 1-tile floor footprint looks natural rather than stretched.

How the code fakes depth. Two tricks complete the illusion. Feet-anchoring: every object's position is where it touches the floor, so its sprite grows upward from a believable ground point (this is the anchor convention fixed in today's scale pass -- previously characters were centre-anchored and floated). Draw order (y-sort): things lower on screen draw later, so a person standing "in front of" a desk correctly covers it. Anchor + draw order is the entire 3D engine of a 2D office.

The one-way door. Perspective is not a setting; it is drawn into every sprite. Switching to true top-down, side view, or isometric means regenerating the entire library -- every character, prop, tile, and animation frame. Palette can be shifted cheaply, scale constants are one-line edits, but the camera angle is close to a full restart of the art programme. If a different angle ever tempts you, prototype it on three sprites in the sandbox before deciding anything. There is one honest escape hatch -- cheating the angle for a single entity family rather than the scene -- and section 6 is entirely about it.

6. Perspective graduations -- the elevation dial

Added after Pip's 2026-07-26 leaning: cats may deserve main-character pixel complexity, humans derived from cats -- "even if it means we make the humans subtly different in how we scale them" -- because a top-down cat loses "way too much potential cuteness, animation detail". This section is the tourist-to-informed-tourist tour of what that instinct is actually about.

The organizing frame: two dials, not one word

What gets mentally filed as "isometricity" is really a small generator. 2D games almost never use true perspective projection. They use parallel projection -- no vanishing points, no size falloff -- which is why a sprite can be drawn once and placed anywhere on screen. That leaves exactly two dials:

  • ELEVATION -- 0° side-on to 90° overhead: how far above the scene the implied camera sits. This is the dial that matters for cuteness, and the one this section walks through.
  • AZIMUTH -- grid square-on (walls horizontal/vertical on screen) vs rotated ~45° so you see two faces of everything and the grid becomes diamonds.

Every named 2D style is a point in that two-dial space. Once you hold the two dials, the zoo of genre labels collapses into coordinates.

The dirty secret: almost nobody uses one consistent angle. Pokemon draws its floors from ~75° and its characters nearly front-on -- two incompatible camera positions in one frame, and no player in thirty years has complained. Per-entity angle cheating is the standard, genre-proven move, and it is the technical basis for treating cats differently from furniture.

The graduations, with the same scene at each stop

90° -- straight overhead (Hotline Miami). Maximum floor-plan clarity, maximum tactical readability; characters become heads with shoulders. Great for violence-at-a-glance, hopeless for charm.

~70-75° -- high top-down, the JRPG cheat (Pokemon, Zelda: A Link to the Past). Floors drawn from nearly overhead; characters drawn nearly frontal and squat (big heads, short bodies). This is the most-cheated view in games -- the dirty-secret box above, as a whole genre's house style.

~45° -- three-quarter, our current "low top-down" (Stardew Valley; all 651 triaged P(Doom) sprites). The management-sim sweet spot: floors still read as area, objects show tops and fronts, characters get taller and more expressive than at 70°.

"Isometric" -- ~30° elevation + 45° azimuth (SimCity 2000, Baldur's Gate, RollerCoaster Tycoon). The grid rotates to diamonds. Game "isometric" is almost always the 2:1 dimetric convention: tile edges run exactly 2 px across per 1 px down -- an angle of atan(1/2) = 26.57° -- because that stair-step is pixel-perfect with no jaggies. The 2:1 ratio was chosen because it renders as clean pixel stairs with no jaggies -- the projection was chosen by the pixels, not the geometry. (True isometric's 30° would need a 1.73:1 ratio that pixels can't draw cleanly.) Handsome, spatial, and expensive: diamond tilesets, more complex draw-order, and usually 8 walk directions instead of 4.

0° -- side-on (every platformer). Maximum character expressiveness -- full profiles, real gaits -- and no floor plan at all: the world collapses to a lane. Wrong genre for an office you manage spatially, but the reference point for what elevation costs you in charm.

Why elevation is the cuteness dial -- especially for cats

The mechanical rule: projection punishes entities in proportion to how horizontal their long axis is. A human is vertical -- even at 75° you still see a readable person, because the head-shoulders-body stack survives the projection. A cat is horizontal -- its identity lives along the axis the camera flattens: the arched back, the tail curve, the head-forward stalk. At 75° it collapses into an ellipse with ears. Elevation IS the cuteness dial -- for quadrupeds specifically. That is why the missing cuteness is not a rendering problem but a projection problem, and no amount of art effort at the wrong elevation buys it back. Walk-cycle detail obeys the same rule: a side-on gait shows legs alternating and the spine flexing; an overhead gait shows a blob oscillating.

"Fits existing library" is the expensive column: a full-scene angle change orphans the 651-triaged sprite library, the 15-promoted tileset set, and the 72 promoted cat-walk frames. That is the number any whole-scene re-angle must argue against.

The cat-anchor decision: three options, ascending cost

(a) Keep the scene angles; cheat the cats one step more side-on. Floors, props, and humans stay at low top-down; cats get regenerated at a visibly lower, more profile-forward angle (PixelLab's view parameter makes this a generation setting, not a hand-redraw). This is the standard JRPG mixed-perspective move applied one entity-family deep, and it orphans nothing -- the rest of the 651-triaged library is untouched. It also matches Pip's own phrasing: humans end up "subtly different in how we scale them" relative to cats, because cats are the ones drawn for charm. Cost: one generation batch (cat families + walk cycles).

(b) ...plus cuteness concentrated in close-ups and portraits. The Hades approach: modest field sprites, gorgeous portraits. The floor cat does its job at floor scale while charm is delivered through the hero layer -- portrait moments, event art, the cat doom-oracle beats, and cat_closeup.png, which already exists. Cost on top of (a): near zero, since the hero pipeline already runs.

(c) Lower the whole scene toward 45° or below and regenerate everything. Maximum coherence, globally more character-forward -- and it orphans the 651-triaged sprite library, the tilesets, and the 72 promoted cat-walk frames. This is the section-5 one-way door taken deliberately: a restart of the art programme.

Recommendation: (a), possibly plus (b) -- essentially all the cuteness for roughly 1/20th the cost. Elevation is the cuteness dial for quadrupeds, so close-ups alone can't fix the floor cat; per-entity cheating is genre-proven invisible and reversible per-family in a way (c) never is. The one discipline it demands: the decision settles before the #900 round-2 mass generation, or the walk cycles and gap-fill sprites get generated twice. Write the ruling into the art direction doc as one sentence -- "cats are drawn one graduation more side-on than the scene; humans hold the scene angle" -- and the generation pipeline enforces it from then on.

7. Composition and readability at small sizes

Everything above is geometry. This section is the craft layer on top -- how to make a 30-pixel-wide thing legible.

Silhouette first

Below ~48 screen px (section 2), identity is carried by outline shape, not interior detail. The working test: fill the sprite solid black -- can you still tell the water cooler from the filing cabinet, the tabby from the eldritch cat? If not, no amount of interior rendering will save it. This is the first gate when triaging generated sprites, ahead of "is it pretty".

Palette density scales with size

A 34 px cat has maybe 300 visible pixels; spend them on 4-6 well-separated colours and it reads instantly. The same sprite rendered with 20 subtle shades turns to noise -- adjacent low-contrast pixels blur together at ~3 arcminutes each. Rule of thumb: the smaller the sprite, the fewer and more contrasty its colours. Save gradient subtlety for the hero library where a portrait gets 400 px of height. This is the mechanical basis of the fidelity split: in-game-cozy = few colours, strong silhouettes, 68 px grid; hero-hardcore = full rendering for close-ups where the eye has pixels to spend.

Contrast against the floor

Characters live on top of floor tiles, so their palettes must separate in value (light/dark), not just hue, from carpet and lino mid-tones -- value is what survives small sizes and peripheral vision. The cheap insurance is the genre's 1 px dark outline around characters: it buys separation from any floor without per-floor tuning, and it is part of the cozy house style already.

Cluster, don't scatter

Five props in a tight desk-cluster read as "a workstation" -- one perceptual object. The same five scattered evenly read as clutter and destroy the room's legibility (where can I walk? what belongs together?). Compose the office in groups with gaps: furniture islands, clear lanes. The eye parses the room by its negative space. This becomes doubly true once walk paths exist -- lanes are where the cats and staff will visibly travel.

Animation is detail you get for free

A feature too small to draw can still be shown: a 2 px coffee steam wisp that moves reads clearly even though a static 2 px wisp would be invisible. Motion is the highest-value channel at small sizes -- which is why the walk cycles and idle fidgets planned for the office floor will add more perceived life than any amount of static detail.
