## 1. THE REFRAME (Ruling 1) -- the doom field, not the military branch

The proposal asked: is diving into military a winning branch or a trap? Pip
declined the frame. The military is not a player build option to be priced --
it is a **set of game actors with legitimate strategic preferences**, who at
some point start interacting with EACH OTHER.

Pip, verbatim:

> "We step back and think of The Military as a set of game actors who have
> legitimate strategic preferences. At some point, they might need to start
> interacting with EACH OTHER. The lab is not the sole or necessarily primary
> target. But when drones start flying, the lab might be a target, collateral,
> or Weird Other. So the arrival of military deployment heralds the endgame in
> many ways, but might not actually be the sole arbiter of final Doom."

Three consequences fall straight out:

1. **The lab loses its centrality in the endgame.** The player is a target,
   collateral, or Weird Other -- not the protagonist of the violence. This is
   a strong break from any tower-defence reading.
2. **Violence heralds the endgame without arbitrating it.** Onset is a phase
   marker, not a win/loss condition.
3. **Actors need strategic desires, not aggro scores.** What the military
   WANTS is the generator; who it shoots is a consequence.

### 1a. The doom field (the generator)

Pip's model, by analogy to Stross's nightmare-stack sequence: as the magic
field builds up, weirdness leaks in at every angle -- eldritch horrors,
dimension-walking elves, superheroes.

> "as doom field builds up, events start emerging."

So the endgame is not a mechanism. It is an **emergence pressure** with a
three-axis texture for classifying what leaks in:

| Failure mode | Obviousness | Simmability | Weirdness | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Military | most obvious | most simmable | least weird | the entry rung |
| Economics | second | easily simmable | very low | **sensitive** -- personal-tragedy content, handle with care |
| Orbital datacenters pacifying the electorate | low-obvious | medium-simmable | **high** | the target |

Pip's worked example of the high-weirdness rung, verbatim:

> "Orbiting data centers sending down waves of pacifying sexbots to get the
> population to vote for their takeover of Ohio is low-obvious,
> medium-simmable, high-weirdness that can arise out of the SAME STRATEGIC
> DESIRES that the Datacenters and Military have when they care about The Vote
> In Ohio."
>
> "We build towards *that*."

**The load-bearing insight:** the weird outcome and the boring outcome are
generated by the SAME strategic desire held by the same actors. Weirdness is
not a separate content pool bolted on at high doom -- it is what ordinary
actor preferences look like once the field is strong enough to let them act
without constraint. An actor that cares about The Vote In Ohio sends
lobbyists early and sexbots late. Same want, different field strength.

This also resolves the Wave-2 anti-rigidity ruling ("multiple likely
end-game-state clusters... don't get tied down in particular endgame
mechanisms"): **the clusters ARE the actor-desire space sampled at different
field strengths.** No cluster needs authoring as a mechanism. Violence is one
cluster, and specifically the least weird one.

### 1b. What this does NOT rule

The proposal's lead-time mechanic (dive = trap / hedge = negative-EV insurance
with a real tail) was neither ratified nor rejected -- it was made a
*second-order* question, because it prices the player's posture against an
actor model that does not exist yet. It survives as a candidate for how the
player relates to the military cluster, once the actors have desires.
**Do not build it as the primary frame.**

### Ruling 2c -- the attention stack (Pip's mechanism)

> "In a violent world, I have to emergency-evacuate my staff, tell people to
> prepare bunkers, etc etc, which are all immediate and that draws my
> attention away from the slow and medium boiling not-very-emergencies that
> will ALSO kill me. See how it stacks?"

The endgame's attention cost is **not** a flat tax. It is a
**salience-inversion**: violence generates loud, immediate, obviously-correct
demands that crowd out the quiet slow-boiling threats which are also lethal.
The player is not made poorer in attention; they are made to spend it on the
wrong thing, correctly, under pressure.

This composes with Ruling 5 (stealth endgame must exist): a low-visibility
build is not exempt from the endgame, it is exempt from the LOUD demands --
and therefore keeps the attention to service the quiet ones. That is the
stealth build's actual payoff, and it falls out of the mechanism rather than
needing a separate reward.

## 3. The violence register (Ruling 6, explicit)

Permitted texture: tentacles, spikes, lightning, runes, special effects,
glowing eyes, floating, Dragon Ball Z style power-ups, spooky effects.
Green and blue liquids are fine.

For broken / defeated challenges and enemies-overcome indications, the
convention is the **German censorship style** (the historical
German-market practice of substituting non-blood fluids and mechanical damage
for gore).

**Never:** blood, gore.

This extends the existing spooky-removals register to state violence:
black-bag yes, on-screen gore never.

## 4a. The actor want-graph (Pip, 2026-07-29 0951)

Wants, as dumped. These are the generator the Section 1 reframe requires.

| Actor | Wants | Denominated in |
|---|---|---|
| **Militaries** | to stay in control of the military within their State | force, legitimacy |
| **Governments / States** | strong State control, expressed through legislation and policy | policy, permits |
| **Electorates** | (they vote for Governments, who produce policy) | votes, driven by public sentiment |
| **Datacenters** | funding, places to be built, customers for compute, water, gigawatts | money, land, water, power |
| **Frontier labs (incl. the player)** | capability; and under restrictive policy or their own fear, security | compute, sentiment, security posture |

Compute is the universal want: **everyone wants datacenters because they
produce Compute.** Datacenters cost **Time, Space and Water** to build, and
whoever controls one generates profit.

### The chokehold

Water and gigawatts are **supplied and controlled by the State**, as is
permission to build. Land gets harder to secure as the game progresses, as it
does in reality. So the State holds a chokehold on the universal want.

### CRUX 1 -- the drone arms race (Pip: "here is a crux!")

1. Policy turns restrictive, OR a lab's own sense of doom rises.
2. Lab takes "hire security drones" options.
3. Governments and States observe drone numbers going up.
4. Arms-race dynamics trigger -> more State control -> back to (1).

Positive feedback. **The player's own defensive action is an input to the
thing that endangers them.** This is the desperation-lever shape lifted to
world scale, and it supplies a mechanism for the proposal's previously
asserted claim that "your protection accelerates the thing killing everyone".

Story dodge, ratified as good-enough: militaries vs datacenter owners drives
the rise of PMCs; frontier labs route around employing human PMCs by using
**remote operators** instead. Pip: *"a flimsy excuse IRL but good enough for
the story."*

### CRUX 2 -- the supply loop (implied, not yet named by Pip)

Sentiment -> votes -> Government -> policy -> water/power/land -> compute ->
capability -> doom. **The player sits on the sentiment lever** (bloggers and
the like, growing with influence). The lategame lever is therefore UPSTREAM:
you do not fight the drones, you influence what permits the gigawatts.

### Wants evolve; the Network is the disclosure gate

Pip: *"as the game progresses, people's wants will evolve over time, so they
don't all need to be surfaced to the player at the same time -- a beginning
player can't even think to influence a vote until a politician is inside their
Network."*

One system does three jobs: onboarding disclosure, midgame expansion, and
lategame lever access. An actor's wants become legible and actionable when the
player's Network reaches them.

### Industry -- the missing supply tier (Pip, 2026-07-29 0955, "expansive thoughts")

> "There is also some other element that's probably something like 'industry'
> that manufactures things for data centres, cpu's, gpu's, and drones...? in
> time, also? We might end up buying or owning these also. 'your gpu factory
> made more gpu's!' gets kind of clickery but also rampy in a way that feels
> intuitively fun? you get reports across your desk if you get more tycoony?"

[CLAUDE-note] Industry is **structurally load-bearing, not optional flavour.**
Without it, drones and compute both materialise out of money alone, and CRUX 1
(the arms race) has no material constraint -- only a financial one. Industry is
what makes an arms race a matter of *production capacity* rather than budget.
It also gives the State a second chokehold (Space, and the inputs to fabs).

**The tension to resolve before this is built** -- the tycoon instinct is real
fun, but its mechanism is *unopposed accumulation*, which is precisely what
this game's spine just removed. The attention economy (AP pool killed) makes
scarcity of attention the central constraint; a factory ramp would be the only
system that produces satisfaction without costing attention proportionally.
Left unexamined, it is a second game bolted to the first.

**Proposed dissolution (needs Pip's ruling):** treat Industry as an ACTOR with
wants, consistent with Section 1, rather than as a player-owned ramp:

- Industry wants demand, materials, energy, and stable policy.
- Player ownership becomes ONE way of relating to it, not the default.
- **If the player owns it, the reports ARE the cost.** Pip's own "reports
  across your desk" instinct is the honest version: a factory you own writes
  to you, and reading it competes with the slow-boiling threats that will also
  kill you. The ramp is thereby converted into an input to the Ruling-2c
  attention stack instead of an exception to it.
- Vertical integration becomes a real strategic choice with a doom-relevant
  price: owning your GPU supply insulates you from one chokehold (though water
  and gigawatts remain State-controlled), while making you a larger arms-race
  input and a larger target.

This keeps the fun Pip identified and denies it a free ride.

### The time-travel network reset (intro)

Time travel justifies reducing the player's Network to **zero** at game start.
Pip's staging: after time travelling the player is hit by a bus and knocked
out; people help them up -- and those people become the first Network
contacts, woven into the intro pitch. Ties into the
antagonist-runs-off-with-two-bags beat as placeholder.

[CLAUDE-note, structural] This closes the CROSSOVER loop: the player travels
back, plays forward through curated real history, and reaches CROSSOVER when
the game catches up to now. The bus is also load-bearing beyond flavour -- a
time traveller with intact foreknowledge is a power fantasy, so the knock-out
is the amnesia justification that keeps the historical stretch honest.
Confirm this reading with Pip before it hardens.
